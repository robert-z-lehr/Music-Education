# Teaching Guide

## Default remote lesson shape

A 30–45 minute beginner lesson can use the same five-part structure each week.

### 1. Perform

Begin with something the student can already play or imitate. Keep the opening musical rather than administrative.

### 2. Diagnose

Choose one thing to improve. Avoid correcting every issue at once.

Useful diagnostic categories:

- pitch
- rhythm
- tempo
- dynamics
- articulation
- phrasing
- fingering
- hand position
- coordination
- tension/relaxation
- listening

### 3. Decompose

Temporarily isolate the relevant variable.

Examples:

- tap the rhythm without notes
- play only the pitches with flexible rhythm
- play one hand alone
- reduce the phrase to 2–5 notes
- remove pedal
- play only the bass motion
- sing the melody before playing it

### 4. Reconstruct

Use a tempo where the student can control the movement. After a correction, repeat the corrected version several times before reconnecting the phrase.

### 5. Explore

End by changing something intentionally.

Examples:

- transpose the first three notes
- change the dynamics
- make a four-note answer to the teacher's phrase
- add a simple bass note
- improvise with a limited note set
- identify the scale degrees used in the melody

## Lesson 1: Make a Melody

### Goal

The student leaves the first lesson able to play at least one recognizable phrase with deliberate rhythm and fingering.

### Opening repertoire sampler

Prepare roughly 6–10 short, recognizable melodies across categories such as:

- children's songs
- folk melodies
- movie themes
- game themes
- famous classical themes
- simple pop melodies

Play only enough of each for recognition. The student chooses a melody to develop.

### Teach immediately

- keyboard geography
- high vs. low
- groups of two and three black keys
- finger numbers
- basic relaxed hand position
- short-phrase imitation
- steady pulse

### Reading

Show the notation for a phrase only after the student knows how it sounds and can imitate at least part of it.

Core sequence:

**Hear → Imitate → Understand → Read → Reproduce without the model**

Later, reverse the flow:

**Read → Imagine the sound → Play → Evaluate by ear**

---

## Rhythm as a separate skill

Do not treat correct pitches as equivalent to playing the melody.

Practice rhythm independently through:

- tapping
- clapping
- counting
- stepping the pulse
- speaking rhythm syllables if desired
- matching teacher-played rhythms by ear

Then restore pitch.

## Reading progression

### Early

- staff direction
- repeated notes
- steps and skips
- treble clef
- basic note values
- simple measures

### Next

- bass clef
- intervals on the staff
- time signatures
- rests
- accidentals
- key signatures

Avoid making letter-name identification the sole reading skill. Encourage intervallic and directional reading.

---

## Harmony progression

A useful order is:

1. intervals
2. major/minor triads
3. I, IV, V
4. inversions
5. melody + chord symbols
6. seventh chords
7. ii–V–I
8. voice leading
9. secondary dominants
10. tritone substitution
11. altered dominants and extensions

### Tritone substitution

A dominant seventh chord may be replaced by another dominant seventh chord whose root is a tritone away. The two chords preserve the characteristic guide-tone tritone while often producing smooth chromatic bass movement.

Teach this after the student understands dominant seventh function and ii–V–I motion.

## Scales, keys, and circle of fifths

Teach scales as explanations of repertoire rather than isolated calisthenics.

Example question:

> Which seven notes supply most of this melody and harmony?

Suggested sequence:

- whole and half steps
- major scale structure
- C, G, and F major
- key signatures
- relative minor
- minor-scale variants
- scale fingering
- arpeggios
- circle of fifths

The circle becomes useful after students already understand what keys and key signatures are.

---

## Modes

Do not begin by demanding memorization of seven mode names and formulas.

Introduce modes when a piece or improvisation makes the sound useful.

Good early candidates:

- Dorian for minor/modal jazz, funk, and improvisation
- Mixolydian for dominant-centered rock, blues, and jazz sounds
- Aeolian as natural minor

Introduce Lydian, Phrygian, and especially Locrian as repertoire creates a reason for them.

Core explanation:

> Use a familiar pitch collection, but make a different note behave like home.

## Continuous strands

These should appear throughout the curriculum rather than becoming isolated advanced units.

### Ear training

- higher/lower
- same/different
- match a note
- reproduce a 2–4 note phrase
- recognize scale direction
- identify major/minor quality
- hear bass motion
- later, recognize intervals and chord qualities

### Improvisation

- black-key improvisation
- question and answer
- one-hand improvisation over a teacher accompaniment
- rhythmic variation of a known phrase
- limited-note composition
- later, chord-tone and mode-based improvisation

### Technique

- relaxed posture
- neutral wrist
- efficient finger motion
- rotation and weight transfer when appropriate
- coordinated release
- avoiding unnecessary tension

Technique should serve sound rather than become a detached gymnastics program.

---

## Branches after the common foundation

### Classical

Reading, technique, repertoire, interpretation, voicing, pedaling, form.

### Pop

Chord symbols, accompaniment patterns, inversions, singing while playing, arranging.

### Composition

Motif, phrase, harmonic support, repetition/variation, form, texture.

### Improvisation

Call and response, pentatonic material, chord tones, rhythmic development, modal playing.

### Jazz

Seventh chords, ii–V–I, shell voicings, guide tones, swing, lead sheets, modes, substitutions.

### Film/game music

Ostinatos, texture, register, harmonic color, arranging, expressive pacing.

### Ear playing

Melody finding, bass recognition, chord-quality recognition, transcription, transposition.
